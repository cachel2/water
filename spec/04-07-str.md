# 4.9 · `str`

**`str` is `[]const u8` with an invariant: valid UTF-8.** Maintained by every operation the language provides:

- **String literals are `str`.** Because `str` is const, writing through a literal — UB in C, and one of its most reliably-hit — does not compile.
- **Indexing yields `u8`.** `s[0]` is a byte; bytes cannot break UTF-8.
- **Slicing validates and traps.** `s[i..j]` traps unless `i` and `j` are both codepoint boundaries. The check is **O(1)**: a byte at index `k` is a boundary iff `k == s.len` or `(s[k] & 0xC0) != 0x80`, and because `s` is already valid, cutting at two boundaries always yields valid UTF-8. Two byte loads and two compares, forever.
- **Construction validates.** `str::from_utf8([]const u8) -> Option<str>` is the only way to make a `str` from bytes.

**The one way to break it, honestly:** `const` is not an aliasing guarantee (§4.3), so aliasing `[]u8` behind a `str` and writing through the bytes leaves the `str` pointing at non-UTF-8. The consequence is **wrong output, never memory unsafety**, and it stays out of the UB budget by one normative rule: **`std` never trusts the UTF-8 invariant for memory safety** — every decoder bounds-checks every byte regardless of what a lead byte claims.

There is no `String` type; a growable string is heap you manage. `str` is not NUL-terminated; `str::to_cstr(s, buf) -> *u8` writes into a buffer you own.
